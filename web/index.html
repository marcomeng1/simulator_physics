      Choosing a physics simulator: a short primer

      A condensed summary of Manda Robotics' deep dive into simulation frameworks for robotics (30 September 2026), with what this rerun adds. There is no overall best engine: the choice depends on your assets and workflow, speed, accuracy for the task, and how much tuning you can afford.

      Three layers that are often confused

      Simulator / framework
      Isaac Lab · Genesis World · Gazebo · MuJoCo Playground

      What you build tasks in: loads robots and scenes, runs many environments, renders sensors, connects to training code. Many frameworks can swap engines.

      Physics engine
      MuJoCo · MuJoCo-Warp · Newton · Genesis · PhysX · Drake

      Computes how things move and collide. This site compares only this layer.

      Renderer
      camera images

      Independent of the physics: a scene can look realistic and still behave unrealistically. All replays here share one viewer.

      What an engine does every timestep

      1. Collision detection: which shapes touch, and where. What you see is often not what collides; meshes become convex hulls, and each importer does this differently.
      2. Contact model: hard (non-penetration, PhysX), soft (small spring-like penetration, MuJoCo's solref/solimp, Genesis), penalty, or pressure-field (Drake's and Newton's hydroelastic). Friction is a cone, often approximated by a pyramid.
      3. Constraint solver: finds all contact and joint forces at once (PGS, TGS, CG or Newton's method). Too few iterations cause jitter and sinking.
      4. Integrator: advances the state by one timestep (semi-implicit Euler, RK4, implicit). It sets stability and energy drift; the controller usually runs every N steps (decimation).

      How to choose, in five steps

      1. Start from your assets. MJCF keeps contact parameters, tendons and actuators; URDF cannot express them, so converters drop them or fill in defaults.
      2. Develop on the CPU, train on the GPU. One small world cannot fill a GPU: MuJoCo CPU is fastest for a single scene, GPU engines win from thousands of worlds (Runtime).
      3. Check that your model is honoured. Importers silently ignore what they do not support (engine support).
      4. Set the knobs explicitly. Timestep, integrator, friction cone and solver iterations have different defaults per engine; the same solref and solimp still give different softness (G1 on soft ground).
      5. Validate the task outcome on a second engine before trusting a success rate, especially for clutter, thin parts, twisting grips and soft objects.

      In short: pick the engine your assets and training stack fit, set every physics option explicitly, and confirm the outcomes that matter on a second engine.

      Contact-rich manipulation

      Crowded sweep: 15 objects into a tray

      A paddle sweeps 15 objects across a table on a frozen 12.5 s joint tape. Dots are individual runs with unchanged inputs.

      In short: crowded outcomes are engine-specific; a success rate measured on one engine does not carry over.

      Peg insertion: 3 clearances × 3 approaches × 3 repeats

      A 20 mm, 64-facet peg is pushed into a chamfered bore by a Cartesian impedance controller; a pass needs 19 mm of depth. "Guided" is the article's contact-guided controller.

      In short: geometry decides tight insertion, so pass and jam cases are the same on every engine.

      Controller tuning: does the best push controller transfer?

      A 27-candidate grid (stiffness × damping × push time) picked on three layouts and checked on six held-out ones.

      In short: the picks differ only in damping, and on every engine they pass 4 of 6 held-out layouts, the same as the untuned baseline.

      Pinch and twist: point contacts vs hydroelastic patches

      Two pads squeeze a 0.1 kg puck with 10 N each (μ 0.5) while a torque about the squeeze axis ramps up. With uniform face pressure the puck slips at an analytic torque (0.133 N·m for a 20 mm-radius disc, 0.115 N·m for a 30 mm square), the case where point contacts are weakest and Drake-style hydroelastic contact, available in Newton 1.7 through its own collision pipeline, should help. "+ invweight fix" copies MuJoCo's compiled body_invweight0 into Newton (see the chart note).

      In short: point contacts over-resist twisting by 1.5–1.9×; hydroelastic patches get within 1.2–1.4×, at 20–1,000× the contacts per step.

      Deformable and elasto-plastic bodies

      MuJoCo 4.0 fixes the 3D flex elastic constants, reworks flex contact and adds elasto-plasticity (springyield on joints, a yield strain on solids and shells). A run is invalid from the first non-finite vertex or inverted tetrahedron. Newton and Genesis do not read MJCF flex; which engine supports what is on the MuJoCo 4.0 tab.

      Elastic ball: plate compression

      125-vertex, 384-tetrahedron ball (60 mm, E = 20 kPa) squeezed 12 mm between two plates. "Legacy constants" runs 4.0 with the changelog's E′, ν′ that reproduce pre-4.0 elasticity.

      In short: 4.0's ball is softer than 3.11's by design (the elastic-constant fix), and CPU and GPU now agree within about 5%.

      Elastic ball: Panda grasp across finger effort

      Close, lift 80 mm, hold, lower and open. Cells give the hold lift, or when the mesh first went invalid.

      In short: MuJoCo-Warp 4.0 holds the ball; MuJoCo 4.0 CPU lifts it too but inverts a tetrahedron at first touch, failing the validity rule.

      Plastic hinge spring (springyield)

      A 2 N·m/rad joint spring with a 5° yield is bent to about 9.5° and released. Ideal plasticity rests at peak − yield; an elastic spring returns to 0.

      In short: plastic joints work on MuJoCo 4.0 CPU and GPU; Newton and Genesis load them and stay elastic.

      Plastic ball (3D flex yield)

      The same 12 mm squeeze and 2 N grasp with yield strains 0.01–0.1. Ideal plasticity has no hardening, so the load the ball can hold falls with the yield strain.

      In short: solid plasticity works on MuJoCo 4.0 CPU only; MuJoCo-Warp 4.0 goes non-finite once the solid yields.

      Plastic sheet (2D shell bending yield)

      A PET-like 120 × 20 × 1 mm cantilever strip (E = 2 GPa) is pressed 15 mm by a sphere at 70 mm and released. With yield="0 εb" the shell yields in bending at the clamp and keeps a permanent tip set.

      In short: shell plasticity keeps a permanent set on MuJoCo 4.0 CPU; MuJoCo-Warp 4.0 keeps much less.

      Legged locomotion: Unitree G1 on five terrains

      Unitree's pretrained G1 walking policy (unitree_rl_gym, 12 leg joints, LSTM, trained in Isaac Gym) runs exactly as in Unitree's MuJoCo deploy script: 50 Hz policy, 500 Hz PD, a constant forward command for 10 s. Each foot touches the ground through four 5 mm spheres; the leg collision meshes touch only when the feet sink deep. Terrains are contact-parameter models, the same on every engine: flat (MuJoCo default contact), glass (μ 0.1), sand (soft, damped, 1.5 cm ripples), mud (very soft, overdamped, μ 1.2) and sponge (soft, springy, bumps up to 2.5 cm). The policy is blind and never saw these terrains.

      Why engines part ways on soft ground: a 35 kg block on eight 5 mm spheres (the G1's weight and feet) settles 4.8 / 8.1 / 6.4 mm into sand on MuJoCo 4.0 / Newton / Genesis, and 12.0 / 9.2 mm into mud on MuJoCo 4.0 / Newton, while Genesis lets it fall through the floor. Same friction, solref and solimp, so the engines map soft-contact impedance differently (scripts/probes/soft_contact_probe.py).

      Upgrading from 3.11: same MJCF, same options

      Per-variant table

      In short: a safe upgrade; the only differences come from contacts on resting box faces.

      Speculative contacts: a playing card on a 1 mm plate

      An 88 × 63 × 0.3 mm, 1.8 g card is released flat 5 mm above a 1 mm thick plate with a downward speed. Standard collision checks only the poses at each step, so a card that moves more than about half the combined thickness (0.65 mm) in one step ends up past the plate's mid-plane and is pushed out of the far side. 4.0's speculative flag predicts the contact before it happens. It exists only on MuJoCo 4.0 CPU (MuJoCo-Warp 4.0 does not implement it).

      In short: speculative contacts stop thin objects tunnelling, but only on MuJoCo 4.0 CPU and not for a spinning card.

      Fourth-order RK4: a T-handle tumbling about its middle axis

      A free T-handle spun about its intermediate principal axis with no gravity flips over periodically (the Dzhanibekov effect), which makes orientation errors easy to see. 4.0 made RK4 fourth-order for free-body orientations. Error is measured against an independent rigid-body solution.

      In short: for free-flying bodies use RK4 on MuJoCo 4.0 CPU; on GPU, float32 caps it near 0.2°.
